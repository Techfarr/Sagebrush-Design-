Large-Scale Learning Project Recovery

A stalled two-workstream learning program had been running for about two and a half months with zero client-approved deliverables. The recovery work focused first on visibility, then review governance, resource alignment, and a realistic delivery plan.

Public portfolio reconstruction. Original project-management boards, client interfaces, and training systems are proprietary. The visuals below recreate the recovery logic and decision structure without reproducing client materials.
The situation

The problem was not simply “late content.” The delivery system was stalled.

Two departments were moving through separate SME and review cycles, but stakeholders lacked a dependable shared view of what was waiting, who owned the next action, and what had actually been approved. Work was being produced, but approval was not moving.

Recovery sequence

Before → Intervention → Delivered

Before

Work existed. Approval did not.

  • Zero client-approved deliverables.
  • Limited stakeholder visibility.
  • Separate workstreams with independent review cycles.
  • Reviews and decisions accumulated instead of moving.
→
Intervention

Make the work visible, then govern it.

  • Shared source of truth for every deliverable and review stage.
  • Due and overdue review notifications.
  • Defined review and approval expectations.
  • Resource assignments reset to the revised scope.
→
Delivered

Approvals moved again.

  • Review cycles began completing on time.
  • Hundreds of learning assets moved through delivery.
  • The revised program was completed on the agreed revised timeline.
Recovery principle: adding more production capacity would not solve a review bottleneck. The first intervention was transparency: establish one source of truth, expose ownership and status, then change the rules that governed movement.

Review and approval system

A project tracker became an operating system for the recovery.

A shared Smartsheet structure gave stakeholders direct visibility into status and made review ownership explicit. Automated notifications surfaced due and overdue reviews instead of relying on meeting-by-meeting follow-up.

Governance change: review expectations were made explicit. The recovery work helped define the parameters later formalized in the change order, including how work could continue when an agreed review window passed without feedback.

Visibility

Stakeholders could see every deliverable, current stage, owner, and review status without waiting for a meeting.

Accountability

Due and overdue notifications made the next action visible and time-bound.

Movement

The process stopped treating silence as an indefinite hold and gave the team a defined path forward.

Resource alignment

The staffing model changed when the scope changed.

The original staffing plan assumed the original scope. After the change order reduced the program, staffing was deliberately resized and then tapered as deliverables completed.

Resource decisions considered

  • Role coverage across both workstreams.
  • Performance and reliability.
  • Skill fit for the remaining deliverables.
  • Current workload and capacity.
  • Familiarity with the content and project history.
Management decision: keep the people and capabilities the revised scope actually required, then rebalance assignments continuously as work moved toward completion.
Delivery constraint

No sandbox did not remove the need for practice.

The customer-service workstream required learners to practice a complex system workflow, but the project did not have access to a safe training environment. The solution was a Storyline simulation rather than screenshots or passive explanation.

Constraint

No sandbox access to the production customer-service platform.

→
Design response

Recreate the workflow in Storyline with multiple screens and states, data-entry behavior, branching, conditional logic, and alternate paths.

→
Learner value

Safe practice on the actual decision flow without exposing the proprietary production interface.

Role: designed the simulation approach, managed the development effort, and personally built portions of the Storyline simulation.
Outcome and evidence boundary

The recovery is supported by delivery evidence, not invented business metrics.

What is verified

  • The program had zero client-approved deliverables about 2.5 months in.
  • Approvals began moving in a timely manner after the recovery interventions.
  • The revised program was completed on the agreed revised timeline.
  • Hundreds of learning deliverables moved across the two workstreams.
  • The team reached 44 contributors at peak and was later reduced as scope changed.

What is intentionally not claimed

The exact final deliverable count is not verified, so the public case study uses “hundreds.”

The project value is not published because the remembered estimate was never confirmed in source documentation.

No proprietary project boards, client system screens, or customer-service interface are reproduced here.
